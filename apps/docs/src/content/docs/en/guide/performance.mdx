The numbers in [Lite, Core and Full](/en/guide/variants/) come from deterministic in-repo fixtures and answer one question: which variant costs less. This page answers a different one: what Kiln holds up under real bitrates, a real upstream, and continuous load. The two runs differ in method and in reproducibility, so do not read them as one dataset.

Everything below was measured on v1.1.0. That release fixed upstream TLS suite negotiation, the options passed to the ffmpeg fallback, and the per-host connection pool ceiling; these measurements were taken after those fixes.

## One hour of continuous dual-channel playback

Two encrypted DASH live sources were consumed simultaneously for one hour: a 4K channel at roughly 15 Mbps on the host loopback, and a 1080p channel at roughly 5 Mbps across an internal tunnel. The consumer was ffmpeg performing a full fetch and demux, not a single manifest request.

| Metric | Value |
| --- | --- |
| 4K channel | 3551 seconds of media decoded in 3600 seconds of wall clock |
| 1080p channel | 3519 seconds |
| Buffering and underrun events | 0 |
| CPU | 3.07% mean, 13.9% peak (123 samples) |
| Container memory | 106 MiB mean, 27 floor, 241 peak (1 GiB limit) |
| Upstream ingest | 8.76 GB, 19.7 Mbps mean |
| Egress | 8.63 GB, 19.4 Mbps mean |
| Server-side warnings | 0 |

CPU percentages treat one core as 100%. The gap between media duration and wall clock comes from startup and the playlist-edge offset; neither consumer ever fell behind real time.

What the run supports:

- **Ingest and egress are nearly equal** (19.7 against 19.4). On-demand pulling works: Kiln fetches only the tracks a player actually requests rather than every upstream track. Pulling all tracks would put ingest well above egress.
- **Memory is a sawtooth, not a climb.** A 27 MiB floor, a 241 MiB peak and a 106 MiB mean, with no monotonic rise across the hour, is the expected shape for segment buffers plus GC. The peak leaves roughly four times headroom under the 1 GiB limit.
- **Every server-side counter stayed at zero**: slow segments, re-anchors, stalls, session restarts and handshake failures.
- Twelve track pauses and five rewarms occurred, which is normal `on_demand` behaviour.

At the decoder, the 4K path logged exactly 2 `Packet corrupt` entries, both on the same timestamp and therefore one segment boundary; the tunnelled path logged 13, of which 7 were `End of file`, matching tunnel jitter. Neither path was interrupted.

## Viewer fan-out

One channel was consumed by 1, 5, 10 and 20 concurrent clients to test whether upstream pulling grows with the audience.

| Concurrent viewers | Upstream ingest | Egress | CPU | Container memory |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 2.01 Mbps | 1.98 Mbps | 0.79% | 67 to 119 MiB |
| 5 | 4.37 Mbps | 21.2 Mbps | 1.74% | 124 to 156 MiB |
| 10 | 5.75 Mbps | 14.9 Mbps | 1.96% | 150 to 214 MiB |
| 20 | 3.84 Mbps | 72.6 Mbps | 2.27% | 143 to 165 MiB |

**Upstream pulling is independent of viewer count.** Ingest stayed in the same 2 to 6 Mbps band from 1 to 20 viewers, and was in fact lower at 20 viewers (3.84) than at 10 (5.75), while egress over the same period reached 72.6 Mbps.

The server-side counters make the point more directly. `kiln_sessions` stayed at 1 throughout. Upstream segment fetches ran at 4.3 per 10 seconds with 10 viewers, 3.9 per 10 seconds with 20 viewers, and 3 per 10 seconds in the gaps where no viewer was connected but the channel had not yet idled out. All three sit in the same band, which means upstream fetching is driven by the rate the stream is produced at, with viewer count playing no part. Kiln keeps one upstream session per channel and fans it out.

CPU grows sublinearly with the audience, reaching 2.27% of one core at 20 viewers. Memory does not track viewer count at all, and was lower at 20 viewers than at 10.

Every client selected the first rendition in the master playlist (576p50, roughly 2 Mbps including audio), so this demonstrates **same-rendition fan-out**. When viewers spread across different renditions, Kiln pulls one copy of each rendition that is actually requested.

<Aside type="caution" title="Read the egress column as an order of magnitude only">
  Egress swings widely and is lower at 10 viewers than at 5. The clients are unthrottled, each holds a different buffer position, and their fetches bunch together; a 60 second counting window spans only about 7 segment periods, which is too small a sample to smooth that bunching out. Use this column only for the order of magnitude, namely that egress can be more than ten times ingest. It is not a steady-state per-viewer rate and it is certainly not a capacity ceiling. A usable capacity figure needs windows of several minutes, repeated over several rounds.
</Aside>

## Traffic planning

With on-demand pulling in effect, a single viewer costs roughly the bitrate of the rendition they selected:

| Rendition | Per hour | Per month at 2 hours a day |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| About 2 Mbps (576p plus audio) | 0.9 GB | About 54 GB |
| About 5 Mbps (1080p plus audio) | 2.3 GB | About 135 GB |
| About 15 Mbps (4K plus audio) | 6.8 GB | About 405 GB |

In the one-hour dual-channel run, the bitrate estimate predicted 9.0 GB against 8.76 GB of measured upstream ingest, roughly 3% low, with the difference coming from variable bitrate and the ramp-up at the start. The table can be used directly for capacity planning. Multiple viewers on the same rendition of the same channel do not increase upstream consumption.

## Live latency

End-to-end latency is dominated by the upstream segment duration and the playlist depth. On this path, upstream segments are 8 seconds long, Kiln keeps 8 segments in the playlist, it advertises a `TARGETDURATION` of 12 seconds, and the LL-HLS part target is 0.5 seconds, with the player's own startup buffer added on top.

<Aside type="caution" title="No measured latency figure is published here">
  We measured how far the playlist edge trails real time, but the result did not reproduce across runs: the same channel landed anywhere between 7 and 55 seconds on different rounds, and the ordering between the two channels reversed entirely. The value depends both on where the sample falls within the segment cycle and on whether the timestamps the upstream writes into `EXT-X-PROGRAM-DATE-TIME` are accurate, and the latter is outside Kiln's control. Until we have a method that reproduces, no number goes here; measure it on your own path.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="What this data cannot support">
  These are field measurements from one machine, one upstream and one deployment, not a reproducible controlled benchmark. The upstream is a private encrypted source, so no reader can reproduce the same path; do not use these absolute values to compare Kiln against other implementations. The run does not cover concurrent pulling of more than two channels, the cost of the ffmpeg fallback path, soaks longer than an hour, the distribution of cold-start time to first frame, or architectures other than arm64. Every fan-out client ran on the host loopback, so real network TCP behaviour is absent and an audience spread across the public internet will behave differently on the egress side. CPU percentages are tied to this specific CPU and do not transfer across architectures.
</Aside>

### Environment and method

The host was an Apple M4 Mac mini running containers on OrbStack under `linux/arm64`, with the Kiln container limited to 1 GiB of memory. The build under test was v1.1.0.

The upstreams were two encrypted DASH live sources reached through an HTTP forward proxy, with 8 second upstream segments. Every consumer used ffmpeg's `-c copy -f null -`, fetching and demuxing in full without transcoding, so the measurement covers the real segment download and packaging path. The 4K path was consumed on the host loopback to exclude the internal network and served as the primary criterion; the 1080p path crossed an internal tunnel and is reference only, since its jitter includes tunnel quality.

CPU and memory come from the container's cgroup v2 counters (`usage_usec` in `cpu.stat`, and `memory.current`); traffic comes from exact byte counters for the container's `eth0` in `/proc/net/dev`. The `NetIO` field of `docker stats` carries only three significant digits, which at GB scale is not enough to difference across a window, so it cannot be used for short-window measurement.

Each fan-out step waited 75 seconds before counting, so that the clients' catch-up burst was over and they had reached steady state, then counted over a 60 second window, with a check at both ends of the window confirming the clients were still alive. Buffering was judged from both sides: underruns and stalls in the client ffmpeg logs, and Kiln's own counters for slow segments, re-anchors, stalls, session restarts and handshake failures.

### Reproducing this on your own deployment

The path above cannot be reproduced directly, but the equivalent measurement runs against your own channels. The repository ships `scripts/live-performance.sh`, which starts an instance and measures cold start, time to the first manifest, throughput as a ratio of real time, peak RSS and peak CPU for the channels you name:

```bash
KILN_PERF_CONFIG=configs/local.toml \
KILN_PERF_CHANNELS=my-channel \
KILN_PERF_CAPTURE_SECONDS=60 \
  sh scripts/live-performance.sh
```

There is no packaged script for soak and fan-out, but the method is not complicated: consume your own channel with ffmpeg's `-c copy -f null -` while sampling the container's cgroup counters at a fixed interval. What matters is waiting for steady state before opening the counting window and confirming the consumers are still alive at both ends of it, otherwise you measure the catch-up burst instead of the steady state.
